## Why does a judgment against a person affect a property?

A money judgment is entered against a person, not a parcel. But once it is recorded in the county's official records, Florida law can give it a lien on real property the debtor owns in that county.

The mechanism is recording. Under [Fla. Stat. § 55.10](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0055/Sections/0055.10.html), a judgment, order or decree becomes a lien on real property in any county when a **certified copy** of it is recorded in that county's official records, provided the recorded copy carries the address of the person who has a lien as a result of the judgment, or an affidavit supplying it is recorded at the same time. Record it in Broward and it reaches the debtor's Broward real property; it does not reach a parcel in Palm Beach until it is recorded there too.

A lien recorded on or after 1 July 1994 runs for **10 years** from the date of recording, and may be extended for a further 10 years by re-recording a certified copy before the original lien expires, with a current address affidavit recorded at the same time. No extension carries the lien past the outer limit in [Fla. Stat. § 55.081](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0055/Sections/0055.081.html) — **20 years** from the date the judgment was entered — or past satisfaction, whichever comes first.

The practical consequence is that the judgment has to be addressed before the property can be conveyed free of it.

## "The seller's agent says not to worry about it."

That is not a status report. It is a hope. Three things have to be established, and none of them is established by someone's confidence:

1. **Is this the same person?** Judgment indexes match on name. A common name produces hits that belong to someone else entirely, and a search that returns a "John Smith" judgment says nothing until identity is confirmed or ruled out.
2. **Does it attach to this property?** That depends on where the judgment was recorded, when, what the debtor owned at the time, and whether the property is protected homestead.
3. **What is the current payoff?** The face amount of a judgment is rarely what it takes to release it. Interest runs, and costs may have been added.

Until those three are answered in writing, "don't worry about it" is not information.

## What about homestead?

Florida's constitutional homestead protection can shield a homestead property from a judgment lien, but it is narrower and more technical than it sounds in conversation, and it does not resolve itself simply because someone lives there.

The protection is constitutional. [Article X, section 4](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Constitution#A10S04) of the Florida Constitution exempts homestead property owned by a natural person from forced sale, and provides that no judgment, decree or execution shall be a lien on it. The protected area is up to one-half acre of contiguous land inside a municipality, or up to 160 contiguous acres outside one.

The same sentence carries the exceptions. The protection does **not** apply to:

- taxes and assessments on the property;
- obligations contracted for the purchase, improvement or repair of the property;
- obligations contracted for house, field or other labour performed on the property.

So a mortgage, a construction lien and unpaid property taxes all reach a homestead. An ordinary money judgment does not.

Two things follow that matter at a closing. First, homestead status is a question of fact — residence, ownership by a natural person, acreage — and it is not established by the property appraiser's tax exemption, which is a different test under a different part of the constitution. Second, a judgment that does not attach still sits in the official records as a cloud, and the buyer's policy has to deal with it. [Fla. Stat. § 222.01](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0200-0299/0222/Sections/0222.01.html) provides a statutory route: the owner records a notice of homestead describing the contract of sale or loan commitment, the clerk serves it on the judgment lienor by certified mail, and a lienor who does not sue to foreclose or bring a declaratory action within **45 days** of service is deemed not to have a lien as to a buyer or lender who takes under that contract within **180 days** of the notice being filed.

If homestead is the reason a judgment is not being paid at closing, that position needs to be documented and accepted by the underwriter before closing, not asserted afterwards.

## How does it get cleared?

Most often it is paid from the seller's proceeds at closing, and the creditor records a satisfaction. That requires a written payoff from the judgment creditor or their counsel — good through a specific date, with the figure and the wiring or delivery instructions confirmed directly with the source.

Where the judgment does not belong to this seller, the route is different: an affidavit establishing that the debtor is a different person, in a form the underwriter will accept.

The instrument is an affidavit of identity — often called a same-name or not-the-same-person affidavit — sworn by the seller, stating that they are not the person named in the judgment and setting out the facts that show it. What makes it acceptable is the supporting detail rather than the denial: full legal name and any former names, date of birth, the last four digits of the social security number, residence history covering the period of the judgment, and a comparison against whatever identifying information the judgment and its underlying case file contain.

Where the match is close, or the judgment is large, the underwriter may also want a search of the debtor's identifiers, a credit report, or a written statement from the judgment creditor's counsel that the seller is not their debtor.

The form and the supporting documentation are First American's call, and we obtain their written sign-off on the affidavit before closing rather than relying on it afterwards.

## When should an attorney be involved?

If the seller disputes the judgment, if it arises from a case that is still active, if it involves a marital or support obligation, or if the homestead question is genuinely contested — those are legal questions with legal consequences. A Florida real estate attorney or the seller's litigation counsel should be handling them.

## Common questions

### Can the buyer just take the property subject to the judgment?

They can agree to almost anything, but they would be buying the lien along with the house, and a lender will not permit it. It is very rarely a sensible outcome for a buyer.

### The judgment is old. Does it expire?

Judgment liens in Florida have a limited life and can be extended by re-recording.

A lien created by recording a certified copy on or after 1 July 1994 lasts **10 years** from the date of recording, and can be extended once for a further 10 years by re-recording before it lapses, under [Fla. Stat. § 55.10](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0055/Sections/0055.10.html). The outer limit is 20 years from the date the judgment was entered, under [Fla. Stat. § 55.081](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0055/Sections/0055.081.html).

An expired lien is not the same as a satisfied judgment. The debt may still be enforceable, and the recorded document is still in the chain — so an old judgment is examined rather than ignored.

### Does a judgment against only one spouse attach?

It depends on how title is held and whether tenancy by the entireties applies.

Where Florida real property is held by a married couple as tenants by the entireties, a judgment against **one** spouse alone does not attach to it. The couple hold a single, undivided interest, and a creditor of one of them cannot lien, levy on or force the sale of that interest while both are alive and married. A judgment against **both** spouses does reach it.

Florida presumes entireties ownership where property is conveyed to a husband and wife who were married at the time and the six unities are present — possession, interest, title, time, survivorship and marriage. The Florida Supreme Court set out the presumption and the burden it places on a creditor in *Beal Bank, SSB v. Almand & Associates*, 780 So. 2d 45 (Fla. 2001).

The protection is not permanent. It ends on divorce, and on the death of the non-debtor spouse the survivor holds the whole estate with the judgment now against the sole owner. Where entireties status is the reason a judgment is not being paid at closing, the position is documented and put to the underwriter before closing, and where it is genuinely arguable it is a question for the seller's attorney rather than for us.

### Will the title policy insure over it?

The commitment will list it as a requirement to be satisfied. Whether an underwriter would ever insure over a judgment, and on what terms, is an underwriting decision made file by file.
