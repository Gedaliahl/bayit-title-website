## What actually connects a lawsuit to the property?

A suit against a person is not automatically a cloud on title. Something has to tie it to the parcel. The usual mechanism is a recorded lis pendens — a notice recorded in the official records that litigation is pending which could affect title to the property.

[Fla. Stat. § 48.23](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0048/Sections/0048.23.html) is the provision. A lawsuit affects real property **only if a notice of lis pendens is recorded** in the official records of the county where the property lies. The notice has to name the parties, give the date and case number and the court, describe the property, and state the relief sought as to the property.

Its effect cuts two ways. A properly recorded notice puts anyone who acquires an interest afterwards on notice that the outcome may affect them. It also bars enforcement against the property of interests and liens that were unrecorded when the notice was filed — federal tax liens included — unless the holder intervenes in the action within **30 days**.

It does not last indefinitely. A notice is **not effectual beyond one year** from the commencement of the action and expires then, unless the action is founded on a duly recorded instrument or on a construction lien under chapter 713, or the court extends it for good cause. And under § 48.23(3) the court controls and discharges a recorded notice as it would grant and dissolve an injunction — which is the doorway to the discharge procedure described below.

Where nothing has been recorded, a pending lawsuit may not surface in a title search at all. That is worth stating plainly, because a search is a search of the records, not an investigation of the seller.

## Why does a short ownership period matter?

Not because a quick resale is improper — it is not. It matters because there has been less time for the record to settle, and because the examination has to look closely at how the seller acquired.

Four things get a closer read:

- **The deed into the seller.** Form, execution, two witnesses, the acknowledgment, whether spousal joinder was needed on a homestead conveyance, and whether the documentary stamps paid match the consideration recited.
- **Whether the prior transaction was itself cleared.** A recent purchase means a recent commitment. Requirements that were satisfied then should be reflected in the record now — a satisfaction recorded, a release filed, an affidavit recorded. Where the prior file's requirements were cleared on paper that was never recorded, the same requirement reappears on ours.
- **How the seller acquired.** A purchase from a foreclosure sale, a tax deed, an estate or a trustee brings that proceeding's own questions with it. A short hold following one of those is a different examination from a short hold following an ordinary arm's-length purchase.
- **Whether a prior owner has a claim.** Anything suggesting the previous owner disputes the conveyance — a recorded affidavit, a lis pendens, a suit in the docket — moves this from examination to an underwriting question.

A short hold is not itself a defect and we do not treat it as one.

## When should an attorney be involved?

Whenever litigation touches the property. A buyer purchasing from a seller in active litigation needs their own Florida real estate attorney advising them on what they are taking on — and where a lis pendens is recorded, that advice is not optional.

## Common questions

### Can a lis pendens be removed before closing?

There is a process for discharging one, but it is a court matter.

The route is a motion to discharge or dissolve in the action itself, decided under [Fla. Stat. § 48.23](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0000-0099/0048/Sections/0048.23.html)(3), which gives the court the same control over a lis pendens that it has over an injunction.

At the hearing the burden is on the party who recorded it. Where the action is not founded on a duly recorded instrument or a chapter 713 construction lien, that party must show a **fair nexus** between the property and the dispute in the lawsuit — the standard the Florida Supreme Court set in *Medical Facilities Development, Inc. v. Little Arch Creek Properties, Inc.*, 675 So. 2d 915 (Fla. 1996). If the nexus is shown, the notice stands, and the court has discretion to require a bond to protect the owner. If it is not, the notice is discharged.

The other routes are quicker and do not involve a judge: the party who recorded it records a voluntary withdrawal, usually as part of a settlement, or the notice simply expires by operation of § 48.23(2) one year after the action commenced.

Timing is the court's calendar, not the closing's. A contested motion needs a hearing date, and that is not something a closing schedule can accelerate.

### Will an underwriter insure over pending litigation?

The distinction that matters is what the suit asks for.

A suit seeking **money only**, with nothing recorded against the property, is generally not a title matter. It becomes one if it produces a judgment, and a judgment recorded after our insured takes title does not reach a property the seller no longer owns.

A suit seeking **an interest in the property** — specific performance, quiet title, rescission, constructive trust, partition, foreclosure of an equitable lien — is a different question, and a recorded lis pendens makes it a requirement on the commitment rather than a matter for judgment. The ordinary outcomes are that the litigation resolves, the notice is discharged or withdrawn, or the underwriter declines.

Whether First American will insure over a recorded lis pendens at all, and on what terms, is theirs to decide file by file. We do not represent in advance that they will.

### If nothing is recorded, is the buyer safe?

The search will not show it, which is not the same thing. This is one of the places where a buyer's own counsel earns their fee.
